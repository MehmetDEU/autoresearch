# Research questions and design

**Working title.** English-Medium Instruction and Oral–Aural Attrition: An Explanatory Mixed-Methods Study of Engineering Students’ Proficiency Change

**Design.** Explanatory sequential mixed methods (QUANT → QUAL).  
The quantitative phase tests whether institutional English proficiency changes from Preparatory Year Programme (PYP) exit to graduation. The qualitative phase asks students what they attribute that oral–aural change to, then uses a prepared follow-up on lecturers’ L1 use. That follow-up is not a separate research question. It was written in advance because the researcher had observed frequent lecturer L1 in these classrooms over several years and expected it to surface. Lecturers are expected to give only one reason for L1 use: students’ low proficiency. That answer is tied to the Golem effect. Students may still name lecturers’ own English; lecturers are not expected to.

Scores are on a **0–100** institutional scale; PYP pass threshold = **60** (≈ CEFR B1).

---

## Research questions

### RQ1 (Quantitative)

To what extent do students’ institutional English proficiency scores change from PYP exit to graduation after four years of engineering EMI, across Listening, Reading, Writing, Speaking, and Overall?

*Anticipated pattern (synthetic panel for scaffolding):* statistically significant decline in **Listening** and **Speaking**; no statistically significant decline in **Reading** or **Writing** (slight non-significant gains possible from written academic exposure).

Students first. The interview is semi-structured: one open research question, then a prepared follow-up. Do not open with L1.

### RQ2 (Qualitative — students)

To what do students attribute their oral–aural attrition?

**Prepared follow-up (not a separate RQ).** Asked after the open answer, of each student. It was written beforehand from the researcher’s observation of these EMI classes over several years; frequent lecturer L1 was expected as a theme, not discovered by accident.

*Why do you think lecturers use Turkish so often?*

If the student has already raised L1, use it as a probe on what they said. If they have not, ask it anyway, after their open answer, and report it as a planned question.

*Follow-up themes, prepared rather than found:* two are enough. (1) lecturers’ limited English; (2) lecturers’ underrating of students’ English. Content security and the shared L1 sit inside (2), not as a third theme. The open RQ2 may still yield reliance on written technical discourse (including multimodal texts) or peer talk in Turkish. See `RESEARCH_ROADMAP.md`.

### RQ3 (Qualitative — lecturers)

When lecturers use L1, what do they say underlies that use?

*How the sequence is meant to work:* RQ2 stays open, so other attributions can appear first. The student follow-up can yield two accounts: lecturers’ limited English, and underrating of students. RQ3 asks lecturers what underlies their L1 use. From extended observation, they are expected to cite only students’ low proficiency. That single answer is interpreted as the Golem effect. They are not expected to reflect on their own spoken English. If that proficiency is raised, the expected excuse is a passed national test (YDS, YÖKDİL, or a similar ÖSYM examination): multiple-choice grammar, vocabulary, and textual meaning, with no Listening or Speaking. The credential does not evidence oral–aural proficiency for EMI teaching.

*Later attribution, not part of the question wording:*

1. **Lecturer English (not Golem).** Some lecturers translanguage to compensate for their own limited English.
2. **Golem effect (underrating).** Lecturers presuppose that students are not proficient enough for English-medium content. Translanguaging, and overuse of Turkish, becomes the easy and safe way to protect subject knowledge. Shared L1 makes that switch feel legitimate: content should not be sacrificed for English. The treatment that follows is less verbal English, and oral–aural skills attrite.
3. These two motives can co-occur. Do not label every switch to Turkish as Golem. Golem requires the low expectancy about *students*.

*Scope:* a short focused lecturer interview (about five or six questions), not a full second qualitative study and not a statistical expectancy model. See `HOW_WE_TEST_GOLEM.md`.

## What is quantitative vs qualitative

| Strand | Data | Analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Quantitative (this phase)** | Paired institutional proficiency scores (Pre PYP exit / Post graduation) for four skills + Overall | Descriptives; paired-samples *t* (Wilcoxon companion); effect sizes / CIs; one Pre–Post figure |
| **Qualitative (next phase)** | Semi-structured. Students: RQ2, then a prepared L1 follow-up. Then lecturers (RQ3) | Open attribution of oral–aural attrition. Follow-up expected from the researcher’s observation. Golem only if lecturers cite students’ proficiency. |

**Out of the quantitative strand:** underrating gap, TL%, Likert scales, OLS/mediation, and Golem path models. The Golem effect is a qualitative attribution in this study, not a regression or mediation result.

## Analytic sample (quantitative)

- *N* = 120 EMI engineering completers (synthetic panel for method scaffolding; replace with live institutional data).
- Same students at Pre and Post.
- Majors: Mechanical, Chemical, Electrical-Electronics (balanced).
