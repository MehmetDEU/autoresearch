# Golem effect in this study

The Golem effect is the **qualitative name** for lecturers’ underrating of student English. It is not a regression, mediation, or path model in the current manuscript.

## Claim to test in interviews, not in the pre/post scores

Lecturers are asked why they use L1. They are expected to give one reason only: students’ proficiency is too low. That answer is the Golem effect in this study. Students may also say that lecturers’ own English is limited. Lecturers are not expected to say that about themselves.

When their own proficiency is at issue, the expected account is that they have already proved it by a test required for English-medium teaching. In this setting that test is typically YDS or YÖKDİL (ÖSYM): an 80-item multiple-choice examination of vocabulary, grammar, translation, and reading. It has no Listening or Speaking section. A pass therefore does not document the oral–aural English EMI teaching uses. This detail belongs next to lecturer excerpts. It explains the excuse. It is not itself the Golem effect.

Classic source: Babad, Inbar, and Rosenthal (1982), the negative-expectancy counterpart of Pygmalion. In this study the biased treatment is translanguaging, not a generally colder teaching style.

## What is not Golem

Lecturers translanguaging because their **own** English is limited. That is a compensatory motive. Code it separately. The two motives may co-occur in the same lecturer.

## What stays out of the statistics

Underrating gap, TL%, Likert scales, OLS, and mediation were scaffolded on the synthetic panel and then removed from RQ1. Do not revive them unless a later paper explicitly returns to a quantitative expectancy model. See `STATISTICAL_AND_FIGURE_DECISIONS.md`.
